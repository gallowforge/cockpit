# Vesper: the look

**An operations console, built to be trusted.** Vesper answers one question, "does anything need me?", so the design is plain on purpose: one sentence first, the facts behind it, then the detail. It is measured against the tools people rely on every day (Linear, Vercel, Raycast), not against a poster.

**The air.** The look takes its air from the film *Vesper* (Kristina Buožytė and Bruno Samper, 2022): a forest after the collapse, fog between the trees, and the cyan glow of living technology. The ground is a deep green-black (`#060d0a`), surfaces are moss in shadow (`#0e1914`, `#13211a`), hairlines are fog at a fixed alpha (`rgba(176, 214, 188)` at 8.5% and 14%), so they read the same over the ground, a panel or the sky, and the text is fog (`#e4eee6`, `#a9bcae`, `#8fa395`).

**By day.** The second theme is the same forest in morning fog, not a second design. The ground is a pale sage (`#eef2ec`), a panel is the ground raised toward white (`#f8faf6`), the text is forest ink (`#10201a`, `#384b41`, `#465a4f`), and the sky is the same cyan and moss thinned to a wash. The ribbon follows the light: by night its threads add light to a dark sky, by day they are ink laid on a pale one, so a lane keeps the colour of its service instead of burning out. State colours were tuned again for a pale ground (green `#16864a`, amber `#ad6800`, red `#d0392c`, cyan `#0a818e`) rather than reused. Every colour in the stylesheet is a token: night's values sit on the root, day's in one block under `[data-theme='light']`, and no rule below them asks which theme it is in. Vesper follows the device until someone chooses; the control is at the foot of the sidebar, under More on a phone, and in the command bar. The colours named in the rest of this page are the night's.

**A solid console under a sky.** The console is an instrument, so it is solid: a panel is the ground raised one step inside one hairline, the four key figures are one readout strip, the primary button is fog white, and nothing glows behind the data. All the light is kept in one place, the sky: a single still layer at the top of the page that scrolls away with it. Cyan light comes from a source just beyond the top right corner, moss green is spread under it, and a fine grain (an SVG noise tile) keeps either from banding. Every stop eases out and the layer fades at its foot, so the sky has no edge anywhere. It is cool light only: a warm wash over this ground turns to mud.

**The masthead.** The Overview opens with the answer on a stage of its own. On a desktop a ribbon of fine threads pours out of the sky's source and thins to nothing before it reaches the words. Every service owns a lane of it, lit the way that service is: a lane that answers runs from the cyan of the source through the green of "up" to a chartreuse tail, one that is struggling runs amber, one that is down runs red, and one Vesper cannot see is a grey ghost. So the picture is the fleet itself, and a lane that turns can be seen turning. Each snapshot that arrives sends a few glints down the threads, and the collector's last round is typed out, three lines at a time, above the rail. Under the masthead runs the horizon: a hairline that starts in the colour of the answer and ends in the light of the source, with a low bloom where the answer stands on it. The sky dims a little when something is down and further when Vesper cannot see.

**How it is built.** `web/src/ribbon.ts` is the ribbon as plain arithmetic (whose thread is whose, the line it hangs along, how a thread lies across it at any moment), with tests; `web/src/stage.ts` draws it on one canvas, at thirty frames a second, and stops while the tab is hidden. With reduced motion and in screenshots (`?shot`) there is one still frame, the same one every time. A phone has the masthead and the horizon and no canvas. The ribbon and the collector's lines are decoration: `aria-hidden`, out of reach of the pointer, and the page says everything in words without them. Besides them, the only things that move on their own are the live dot, a service that is down now, the day lines drawing in once as a page opens, and a key figure easing to its new value.

**Colour still only means state.** Green (`#58de86`) is up, marigold (`#f7ab3e`) needs you, coral red (`#ff6a5c`) is down, and hatched sage means Vesper cannot see. A down mark that is over turns a quieter red, so only what is down now is loud. Every state also has a shape and a word, never colour alone, and the day lines say it twice: up sits low, degraded rises, down stands full height, so an outage reads without colour. A failed run carries the same bar cut as the down mark. Cyan (`#5fe1ea`) is not a state and keeps to its own job: live, or something you can press (the live dot, focus rings, the active page, links). The ribbon and the horizon are the one place a state colour leaves the marks, and there it means what it always means: a lane is the colour of its service's state, the horizon the colour of the answer. Neither is a mark; each repeats what a mark and a sentence already say.

**Contrast.** Every text colour keeps at least 4.5:1 and every status mark at least 3:1 against the ground and the raised surfaces. Text was also measured against the rendered page, pixel by pixel behind every run of it, with the sky, the ribbon and the grain painted: every page at desktop and phone width, and the Overview in all four demo states. The lowest is 5.2:1. That measurement is the night theme. By day the same limits were checked token against token, for every ground a colour can sit on (the page, a panel, a hovered or pressed row, a well, the sky's wash): the lowest text is 4.7:1 and the lowest mark 3.1:1.

**Honest by construction.** The day line marks time Vesper did not watch as unknown, uptime is counted only over what it saw, and a check it cannot make is a blind spot, never an all-clear. The design shows these states as plainly as the good ones.

**Type.** IBM Plex Sans for words and IBM Plex Mono for every figure, both vendored with their licences, so the console needs nothing from the internet. Numbers are tabular so columns line up.

**Layout.** On a desktop: a sidebar, the masthead with the answer across the top of the Overview, and under it the main column (the 24-hour strip, four figures, the services table) and a rail (since you last looked, backups, the next jobs, the host, what Vesper can see). On a phone: one column and a tab bar with a More tab. Titles and their lines wrap instead of hiding what they say. Every page is checked at phone width for overflow.

**The mark.** A V in fog white under the evening star, the first light of the night, glowing cyan like the living technology of the film. App icons are rendered from `docs/brand/app-icon.html`.

**The README.** The screens are the real UI of `vesper demo` on synthetic data and a frozen clock, staged as glass windows in the same family as the author's GitHub profile. The dark images show the night theme and the light images the day theme, each captured from the real UI and each with a phone layout. In the hero the status lifted out of the Overview turns from "Nothing needs you" to "Home Assistant is down" and back: the one motion, and the product's whole idea.

**Rebuild.** `scripts/readme-visuals.sh` (see its header). Sources: `docs/readme/src/textures.mjs` (captures and typeset windows), `docs/readme/src/layouts.js` (compositions), `docs/readme/src/glass/` (the renderer: three.js in headless Chromium). The detail for a careful viewer is in the demo data: among the real homelab jobs, "Remind the humans to descale the kettle", Saturdays at 09:00.
